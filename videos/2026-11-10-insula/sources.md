Pages used:
- [W] Wikipedia, *Insula (building)*: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insula_(building)
- [H] HistorySkills, *Roman insulae: Deadly risks of living in an ancient apartment building*: https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/year-7/insulae/

| Claim in the script / caption | Source |
|---|---|
| Ground floor held tabernae, small shops opening onto the street, with living space above | [W] "The ground-level floor of the insula was used for tabernae, shops and businesses, with living spaces above." [H] "Ground floors also housed tabernae, which were small shops or businesses that opened directly onto the street." |
| Lower levels in brick-faced concrete; upper levels in timber and mudbrick | [H] "builders had relied on brick-faced concrete for the exterior walls on the lower levels"; "the upper levels were often constructed from timber and mudbrick." [W] "built in timber, brick, and later Roman concrete" |
| Wealthier tenants on lower floors (steadier, nearer the street); upper floors cheapest, poorest at the top | [H] "Because lower floors provided greater stability and easier access to the street, they were reserved for wealthier tenants." [W] "the largest and most expensive apartments being located on the bottom floors"; "the uppermost floors of insulae were the least desirable, and thus the cheapest to rent." |
| Sometimes built cheaply | [W] (Strabo) "this type of housing was sometimes constructed at minimal expense" |
| Upper floors: no kitchens / plumbing; water from public fountains; food from street vendors | [H] "Due to the lack of direct plumbing in most insulae, residents depended on public fountains"; "Because most insulae lacked private kitchens, tenants relied on purchasing prepared meals from street vendors." (Wikipedia notes some luxury Ostia insulae may have had upper-floor kitchens, so the script says "up here", meaning a cheap upper floor.) |
| A Roman poet: buildings "held up by flimsy bits of wood" | [H] "most of the buildings are held up by flimsy bits of wood", as the satirist Juvenal put it. [W] cites Juvenal, Satires 3.193–211, for "prone to fire and collapse" |
| Cicero's rental buildings collapsing | [H] Cicero: "several of his rental buildings had collapsed or were nearly on the verge of doing so." [W] Cicero "happy that he could charge higher rents for a new building than the collapsed one" |
| Great Fire of 64 swept districts of insulae | [H] "the Great Fire of Rome of 64 CE consumed entire districts filled with insulae." |
| Height limit of 70 Roman feet, about 21 m | [H] "the Lex Iulia Municipalis in 20 BCE restricted the height of new buildings to 70 Roman feet, which is approximately 21 meters." [W] Augustus "introduced a height limit of about 70 Roman feet (20.7 m)" |
| Trajan reduced it to 60 (Roman) feet | [W] "Emperor Trajan reduced the height to 60 Roman feet (17.75 m)." |
| Up to about nine storeys; most four to six | [W] "could be built up to nine storeys"; [H] "Most insulae ranged between four and six storeys in height." |
| More than 40,000 insulae vs fewer than 2,000 private houses (late-antique city lists) | [H] "Rome had 46,602 insulae compared to 1797 private houses" (Regionary Catalogues). [W] "about 42,000–46,000 insulae in the city, as compared to about 1,790 domus". Script uses the safe floor: "over forty thousand", "fewer than two thousand". |
