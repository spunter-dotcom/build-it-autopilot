# Sources: "You built a trebuchet" (2026-11-13)

| Claim in the script or caption | Source |
|---|---|
| A long beam pivoting on a frame, with two arms of unequal length; the projectile sits in a sling or cup on the long arm | Britannica, *Trebuchet*: the beam has "two sections of unequal length"; "The end of the long arm was fitted with a sling or a cup to hold the projectile." https://www.britannica.com/technology/trebuchet |
| The first form was pulled by a crowd of people on ropes tied to the short arm (traction trebuchet) | Britannica: "The first and earliest form of the weapon ... was powered by a large group of persons, who launched the projectile ... by pulling on ropes attached to the short arm." Same URL. |
| The idea began in China, roughly 2,000+ years ago (5th–3rd centuries BCE) | Britannica: "The trebuchet first appeared between the 5th and 3rd centuries bce in China." Same URL. |
| Some Chinese traction machines needed crews of up to about 250 pullers | Wikipedia (IPFS mirror), *Trebuchet*, table of Chinese traction trebuchets: crews from 40 to 250 (e.g. "Four-footed seven-arm" 250). https://ipfs.ssi.eecc.de/ipns/en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Trebuchet |
| The counterweight: a heavy box filled with stones or sand, hung on a hinge from the short arm | Wikipedia (IPFS mirror): "an extremely heavy box (typically filled with stones, sand, or lead)" attached "on a hinged connection". Britannica: "a heavy-laden box ... was attached to the short arm to act as a counterweight." |
| Counterweight trebuchets appeared around the Mediterranean in the 12th century | Wikipedia (IPFS mirror): "It appeared in both Christian and Muslim lands around the Mediterranean in the 12th century." |
| Heavy counterweights were wound down with winches or treadwheels; a windlass is recorded on counterweight machines (1165) | Wikipedia (IPFS mirror): "either winches or treadwheels, aided by block and tackle, are typically required to raise the more massive counterweights"; "a windlass, which is only useful to counterweight machines." |
| The sling: one end fixed to the beam, the other a loop over a release pin at the beam's tip; the loop slips off and the sling opens | Redstone Projects, *How a trebuchet catapult works*: "One end of the sling is fixed to the end of the beam, while the other is tied in a loop and slipped over a release pin extending from the end of the beam"; "the loop slips off the pin and the sling opens". https://www.redstoneprojects.com/trebuchetstore/how_a_trebuchet_catapult_works.html |
| The sling effectively lengthens the arm, for a longer throw | Britannica: a longer sling "effectively lengthened the arm". |
| Stones of 200–300 kg ("heavier than three grown men") | Britannica: "trebuchets could launch objects weighing between 440 pounds (200 kg) and 660 pounds (300 kg)." |
| A 60 kg stone thrown more than about 350 m | Britannica: "certain trebuchets were able to launch a 130-pound (60-kg) projectile" more than 1,150 feet (about 350 metres). |
| Gunpowder artillery in the 15th century ("the fourteen hundreds") made them obsolete | Britannica: "After the emergence of modern gunpowder artillery in the 15th century, the trebuchet began to lose its relevance." |

Softened or general wording (no number): "stones land all over the place" with a crowd pulling (narrative framing); the castle wall's height ("taller than five men") is a scene-setting example, not a historical claim.
