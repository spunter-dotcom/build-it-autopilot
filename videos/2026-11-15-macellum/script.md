Your Roman town needs fresh meat and fish, and there is no ice. Out in the open street, it spoils fast. So you wall in a square courtyard and pave it with stone. But the sellers need shade. So you raise a row of columns along the walls. This is a colonnade. Behind it you build rows of small shops, all the same size, and roof it all with clay tiles. But meat warms up fast on a wooden table. So you give the butchers counters of marble, which stays cool, with drains to carry the mess away. But fresh fish needs water. So in the middle of the yard you stack round steps, stand a ring of columns on top, and cap it with a round roof. This is a tholos. Inside, you pipe in a fountain. Here the fish are gutted and cleaned, and the scales wash down a drain. At Pompeii, diggers found fish bones and scales still lying in that drain. The Romans had a name for a food market like this. This is a macellum. The emperor Nero built a grand one in Rome in the year fifty-nine. Congratulations. You have built a Roman food market.
