# You built a water clock

Hook arm A (statement).

Your temple needs to tell time at night.
The sun is gone, and clouds hide the stars.
So you carve a stone bowl and fill it with water.
You drill a tiny hole near the bottom, and it starts to drip.
As it empties, you scratch a mark for every hour.
This is a clepsydra.
But as the water drops, the pressure drops, and the drip slows down.
So you slope the sides, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, so the level falls at a steady rate.
But Egyptian hours stretch and shrink with the seasons.
So you carve twelve columns of marks inside, one for every month.
A thousand years later, Athens uses the same trick in court.
You may speak until the water runs out.
But an emptying bowl will always slow down.
So in Alexandria, an inventor named Ctesibius flips the idea.
Water flows in, not out.
A big tank on top stays full, and an overflow pipe spills the extra.
The steady stream fills a tall jar, and a float rises inside it.
You fix a pointer to the float, and it climbs past the hours.
This is an inflow water clock.
Then you cut teeth into the bar, and they turn a drum.
Congratulations. You have built a water clock.

Voice text: "Ctesibius" read as "teh-sib-ee-us", "clepsydra" as "klep-sih-druh".
