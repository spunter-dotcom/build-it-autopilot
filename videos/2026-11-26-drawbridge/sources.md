# Sources: castle drawbridge

- Medieval castles were usually defended by a ditch or moat crossed by a wooden bridge; a drawbridge is a wooden deck hinged or pivoting at the gatehouse threshold, immediately outside the gatehouse. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawbridge
- Raised or lowered with ropes or chains attached to a windlass in a chamber in the gatehouse above the gate-passage. Only a very light bridge could be raised without a counterweight, so some bascule arrangement is normally found. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawbridge
- The hoisting gear, a geared windlass, sat in the room above the gateway, often the guardroom. https://castlesandmanorhouses.com/architecture_04_gates.htm
- Counterweights on the lifting chains let a windlass turn a pair of lifting drums that gathered in the chains; with counterweights, very heavy bridges could be worked by just a few people. https://www.historyanswers.co.uk/inventions/how-drawbridges-worked/
- By the 14th century, lifting arms ("gaffs") above and parallel to the deck were linked by chains to the bridge; their inner ends carried counterweights. When raised, the gaffs fit slots in the gatehouse wall, still visible at Herstmonceux Castle. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawbridge
- When raised, the bridge sat flush against the gate; it was backed by one or more portcullises and gates. Sometimes the portcullis itself was the counterweight, as at Alnwick. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawbridge ; https://castlesandmanorhouses.com/architecture_04_gates.htm
- A moat and drawbridge made a two-fold barrier. https://www.historyanswers.co.uk/inventions/how-drawbridges-worked/
- In England, 2 working drawbridges remain in regular use at Helmingham Hall, which dates from the early 16th century (early 1500s). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawbridge
- A typical castle had only a single entrance gate. https://castlesandmanorhouses.com/architecture_04_gates.htm
